Bezier Curves

Main reference: Saxena and Sahay [1], Chapter 4. Further reading: Farin [5], Chapters 4–5, and Shene’s university notes [7] for Bézier construction, subdivision and degree elevation.

A Bezier curve of degree $n$ is defined using $n+1$ control points

\[ \mathbf P_0,\mathbf P_1,\ldots,\mathbf P_n. \]

The curve is

\[ \mathbf r(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{n} B_{i,n}(u)\mathbf P_i, \qquad 0\le u\le 1, \]

where $B_{i,n}(u)$ are Bernstein basis polynomials:

\[ B_{i,n}(u)= \binom{n}{i}u^i(1-u)^{n-i}. \]

Example

A quadratic Bezier curve has three control points $\mathbf P_0,\mathbf P_1,\mathbf P_2$ and is given by

\[ \mathbf r(u)=(1-u)^2\mathbf P_0+2u(1-u)\mathbf P_1+u^2\mathbf P_2. \]

Properties

Bezier curves have the following important properties.

  1. Endpoint interpolation:

    \[ \mathbf r(0)=\mathbf P_0, \qquad \mathbf r(1)=\mathbf P_n. \]

  2. Convex hull property: The curve lies inside the convex hull of its control points.

  3. Affine invariance: Applying an affine transformation to the control points gives the same result as applying it to the curve.

  4. Variation diminishing property: A line intersects the curve no more times than it intersects the control polygon.

  5. Endpoint tangent property:

    \[ \mathbf r'(0)=n(\mathbf P_1-\mathbf P_0), \qquad \mathbf r'(1)=n(\mathbf P_n-\mathbf P_{n-1}). \]

Remark

The control polygon gives a visual handle for shaping the curve. Moving a control point changes the shape of the curve.

Derivative of a Bezier Curve

The derivative of a Bezier curve of degree $n$ is another Bezier curve of degree $n-1$:

\[ \mathbf r'(u)= n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}B_{i,n-1}(u)(\mathbf P_{i+1}-\mathbf P_i). \]

Thus, the derivative control points are

\[ \mathbf Q_i = n(\mathbf P_{i+1}-\mathbf P_i), \qquad i=0,1,\ldots,n-1. \]

Key Idea

The derivative of a Bezier curve is controlled by the differences between consecutive control points.

Curve Subdivision

Bezier curve subdivision splits a curve into two Bezier curves at a parameter value $u=t$. The de Casteljau algorithm is used for this purpose.

Starting from control points

\[ \mathbf P_i^{(0)}=\mathbf P_i, \]

define recursively

\[ \mathbf P_i^{(r)}=(1-t)\mathbf P_i^{(r-1)} +t\mathbf P_{i+1}^{(r-1)}. \]

The final point

\[ \mathbf P_0^{(n)} \]

is the point on the Bezier curve at parameter $t$.

The left and right sets of generated points form the control points of the two subdivided curves.

Remark

Subdivision is useful in rendering, intersection calculations, adaptive refinement and geometric modeling.

Degree Elevation

Degree elevation converts a Bezier curve of degree $n$ into an equivalent curve of degree $n+1$.

Let the old control points be

\[ \mathbf P_0,\mathbf P_1,\ldots,\mathbf P_n. \]

The new control points are

\[ \mathbf Q_0=\mathbf P_0, \qquad \mathbf Q_{n+1}=\mathbf P_n, \]

and

\[ \mathbf Q_i= \frac{i}{n+1}\mathbf P_{i-1} + \left(1-\frac{i}{n+1}\right)\mathbf P_i, \qquad i=1,2,\ldots,n. \]

Key Idea

Degree elevation changes the control polygon but not the geometric curve.

Rational Bezier Curves

A rational Bezier curve uses weights $w_i$ associated with control points:

\[ \mathbf r(u)= \frac{ \sum_{i=0}^{n} w_i B_{i,n}(u)\mathbf P_i }{ \sum_{i=0}^{n} w_i B_{i,n}(u) }. \]

When all weights are equal, the rational Bezier curve reduces to the ordinary polynomial Bezier curve.

Weights provide additional shape control. Increasing a weight pulls the curve closer to the corresponding control point.

Homogeneous Coordinates and Rational Curves

Further reading on weighted homogeneous control points and rational curves: Farin [5], Chapter 13; Piegl and Tiller [6], pp. 117–139; and Shene [7].

Rational curves can be understood as projections of polynomial curves in homogeneous coordinates.

For a point

\[ \mathbf P_i=(x_i,y_i,z_i) \]

with weight $w_i$, define the homogeneous point

\[ \mathbf P_i^h=(w_ix_i,w_iy_i,w_iz_i,w_i). \]

Construct the polynomial Bezier curve in homogeneous space:

\[ \mathbf R^h(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}B_{i,n}(u)\mathbf P_i^h. \]

If

\[ \mathbf R^h(u)=(X(u),Y(u),Z(u),W(u)), \]

then the rational curve in ordinary space is

\[ \mathbf r(u)= \left( \frac{X(u)}{W(u)}, \frac{Y(u)}{W(u)}, \frac{Z(u)}{W(u)} \right). \]

Rational Representation of Circle and Ellipse

For exact conic and circular-arc representations, see Piegl and Tiller [6], pp. 281–331, and Shene’s conic-section and circular-arc notes [7].

Polynomial Bezier curves cannot exactly represent a circle. Rational Bezier curves can.

Symmetric Circular Arc

Consider the circular arc of radius \(R\), centred at the origin and symmetric about the positive \(x\)-axis:

\[ \mathbf c(\phi)=(R\cos\phi,R\sin\phi), \qquad -\theta\leq\phi\leq\theta. \]

The arc subtends the central angle \(2\theta\). For \(0<\theta<\pi/2\), its exact quadratic rational Bezier representation has control points

\[ \mathbf P_0=(R\cos\theta,-R\sin\theta), \qquad \mathbf P_1=(R\sec\theta,0), \qquad \mathbf P_2=(R\cos\theta,R\sin\theta), \]

with weights

\[ w_0=1, \qquad w_1=\cos\theta, \qquad w_2=1. \]

The middle control point is the intersection of the tangent lines at the two endpoints. The rational curve is

\[ \mathbf r(u)= \frac{ (1-u)^2w_0\mathbf P_0 +2u(1-u)w_1\mathbf P_1 +u^2w_2\mathbf P_2 }{ (1-u)^2w_0+2u(1-u)w_1+u^2w_2 }, \qquad 0\leq u\leq1. \]

Its image is exactly the stated circular arc. At \(\theta=\pi/2\), the arc is a semicircle: \(w_1=0\) and the middle control is a point at infinity, so it must be treated in homogeneous coordinates. Longer arcs are normally represented with multiple rational quadratic spans.

Affine Invariance of Rational Bezier Curves

Let an affine transformation be

\[ T(\mathbf x)=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b. \]

Apply it to every control point while leaving the weights unchanged:

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf P}_i=A\mathbf P_i+\mathbf b, \qquad \widetilde w_i=w_i. \]

Because the rational basis functions sum to one,

\[ \frac{\sum_i w_iB_{i,2}(u)\widetilde{\mathbf P}_i} {\sum_i w_iB_{i,2}(u)} =A\mathbf r(u)+\mathbf b =T(\mathbf r(u)). \]

Thus an affine image of the circular arc is obtained simply by applying the same affine transformation to its control points. The weights do not change. Rotations and translations reposition the circle; uniform scaling changes its radius; non-uniform scaling converts it into an ellipse.

Quarter-Circle and Ellipse Examples

Quarter circle by rotation

Choose \(\theta=\pi/4\). The symmetric construction initially describes the arc from \(-45^\circ\) to \(+45^\circ\):

\[ \mathbf P_0=\left(\frac{R}{\sqrt2},-\frac{R}{\sqrt2}\right), \quad \mathbf P_1=(\sqrt2R,0), \quad \mathbf P_2=\left(\frac{R}{\sqrt2},\frac{R}{\sqrt2}\right), \]
\[ (w_0,w_1,w_2)=\left(1,\frac{1}{\sqrt2},1\right). \]

Rotate every control point counterclockwise through \(45^\circ\) using

\[ Q_{45}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}. \]

The transformed controls are

\[ Q_{45}\mathbf P_0=(R,0), \qquad Q_{45}\mathbf P_1=(R,R), \qquad Q_{45}\mathbf P_2=(0,R), \]

with the same weights \((1,1/\sqrt2,1)\). They represent the exact first-quadrant quarter circle.

Quarter ellipse by non-uniform scaling

Starting from that quarter circle, apply

\[ S= \begin{bmatrix} a/R&0\\ 0&b/R \end{bmatrix}. \]

The circle \(x^2+y^2=R^2\) becomes the ellipse \(x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2=1\), and the control points become

\[ \widehat{\mathbf P}_0=(a,0), \qquad \widehat{\mathbf P}_1=(a,b), \qquad \widehat{\mathbf P}_2=(0,b), \]

again with weights \((1,1/\sqrt2,1)\). Four rotated quarter-circle spans give a complete circle; applying the same non-uniform scale to all their control points gives a complete ellipse.

Key Idea

Rational Bezier curves are affine invariant: transform the control points and retain the weights. This gives exact rotated circular arcs and exact elliptical arcs without deriving a new rational curve formula.
