Surfaces are two-parameter geometric objects. They are used to represent freeform shapes such as car bodies, aircraft wings, turbine blades, architectural forms and consumer-product surfaces.

Parametric Representation

A parametric surface is represented as

\[ \mathbf r(u,v)= \begin{bmatrix} x(u,v)\\ y(u,v)\\ z(u,v) \end{bmatrix}, \]

where $u$ and $v$ are parameters.

For example, a sphere of radius $R$ may be represented by

\[ \mathbf r(u,v)= \begin{bmatrix} R\sin u\cos v\\ R\sin u\sin v\\ R\cos u \end{bmatrix}, \]

where

\[ 0\le u\le \pi,\qquad 0\le v\le 2\pi. \]

A tensor-product Bezier surface is

\[ \mathbf r(u,v)= \sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{p} B_{i,n}(u)B_{j,p}(v)\mathbf P_{ij}. \]

A tensor-product B-spline surface is

\[ \mathbf r(u,v)= \sum_i\sum_j N_{m,i}(u)M_{\ell,j}(v)\mathbf P_{ij}, \]

where $N_{m,i}$ and $M_{\ell,j}$ are B-spline basis functions in the $u$ and $v$ directions respectively.

Key Idea

A surface is a two-parameter extension of a curve. Curves use one parameter, while surfaces use two.

Differential Geometry of Surfaces

Main reference: Saxena and Sahay [1], Chapter 6. Further reading: Pressley [4], Chapters 6–8, for fundamental forms and surface curvature.

A surface may be represented parametrically as

\[ \mathbf r(u,v)= \begin{bmatrix} x(u,v)\\y(u,v)\\z(u,v) \end{bmatrix}, \]

or implicitly as

\[ f(x,y,z)=0. \]

In CAD, parametric representation is especially important because most surface patches are constructed using two parameters \(u\) and \(v\).

Tangents and Tangent Plane

Let \(P\) be a point on the surface \(S\), and let \(\alpha(s)\) be a curve drawn on the surface passing through \(P\). Suppose \(s\) is the arc length parameter of this curve.

Since \(\alpha(s)\) lies on the surface, it may be written as

\[ \alpha(s)=\mathbf r(u(s),v(s)). \]

Differentiating with respect to \(s\), we get

\[ \frac{d\alpha}{ds} = \frac{\partial\mathbf r}{\partial u}\frac{du}{ds} + \frac{\partial\mathbf r}{\partial v}\frac{dv}{ds}. \]

Using the notation

\[ \mathbf r_u=\frac{\partial\mathbf r}{\partial u}, \qquad \mathbf r_v=\frac{\partial\mathbf r}{\partial v}, \]

the tangent vector becomes

\[ \hat{\mathbf t} = \frac{d\alpha}{ds} = \mathbf r_u\frac{du}{ds} + \mathbf r_v\frac{dv}{ds}. \]

Thus, every tangent vector to a curve on the surface can be written as a linear combination of \(\mathbf r_u\) and \(\mathbf r_v\).

Key Idea

At a regular point of a parametric surface, the tangent plane is spanned by

\[ \mathbf r_u \quad\text{and}\quad \mathbf r_v. \]

The tangent space is denoted by

\[ T_P(S). \]

First Fundamental Form

The first fundamental form describes how lengths and angles are measured on a surface.

For a small displacement on the surface,

\[ d\mathbf r = \mathbf r_u\,du+\mathbf r_v\,dv. \]

The squared differential length is

\[ ds^2=d\mathbf r\cdot d\mathbf r. \]

Therefore,

\[ ds^2 = (\mathbf r_u\cdot\mathbf r_u)\,du^2 + 2(\mathbf r_u\cdot\mathbf r_v)\,du\,dv + (\mathbf r_v\cdot\mathbf r_v)\,dv^2. \]

Define

\[ E=\mathbf r_u\cdot\mathbf r_u, \qquad F=\mathbf r_u\cdot\mathbf r_v, \qquad G=\mathbf r_v\cdot\mathbf r_v. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ ds^2=E\,du^2+2F\,du\,dv+G\,dv^2. } \]

In matrix form,

\[ ds^2 = \begin{bmatrix} du & dv \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} E & F\\ F & G \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} du\\dv \end{bmatrix}. \]

The matrix

\[ \mathbb G= \begin{bmatrix} E & F\\ F & G \end{bmatrix} \]

is called the first fundamental matrix or metric matrix.

Its determinant is

\[ \Delta_I= |\mathbb G| = EG-F^2. \]

Key Idea

The first fundamental form contains all information needed to measure lengths, angles and areas on a parametric surface.

Surface Normal and Area Element

The unit normal vector to the surface is

\[ \hat{\mathbf n} = \frac{\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v} {\|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|}. \]

Using the vector identity

\[ (\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\cdot(\mathbf C\times\mathbf D) = (\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf C)(\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf D) - (\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf D)(\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf C), \]

we get

\[ \|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|^2 = (\mathbf r_u\cdot\mathbf r_u)(\mathbf r_v\cdot\mathbf r_v) - (\mathbf r_u\cdot\mathbf r_v)^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|^2 = EG-F^2 = \Delta_I. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\| = \sqrt{\Delta_I}. } \]

The differential area element is

\[ \boxed{ dA= \|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|\,du\,dv = \sqrt{\Delta_I}\,du\,dv. } \]

Second Fundamental Form

The first fundamental form measures the intrinsic geometry of the surface. To describe how the surface bends in three-dimensional space, we use the second fundamental form.

Let \(\mathbf r\) be a point on the surface and let \(\mathbf r+d\mathbf r\) be a neighboring point. The signed distance of the neighboring point from the tangent plane is, to second order,

\[ d = \frac{1}{2} \left( \mathbf r_{uu}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,du^2 + 2\mathbf r_{uv}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,du\,dv + \mathbf r_{vv}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dv^2 \right). \]

Define

\[ L=\mathbf r_{uu}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}, \qquad M=\mathbf r_{uv}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}, \qquad N=\mathbf r_{vv}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}. \]

Then the second fundamental form is

\[ \boxed{ II=L\,du^2+2M\,du\,dv+N\,dv^2. } \]

The second fundamental matrix is

\[ \mathbb C= \begin{bmatrix} L & M\\ M & N \end{bmatrix}. \]

Its determinant is

\[ \Delta_{II}=|\mathbb C|=LN-M^2. \]

Remark

The coefficients \(L,M,N\) are similar in spirit to second derivatives of a function, but they measure second derivatives of the surface in the normal direction.

Normal Curvature

Let \(\alpha(s)\) be a curve on the surface and let

\[ \hat{\mathbf t}=\alpha'(s) \]

be its unit tangent vector.

The curvature vector of the curve is

\[ \frac{d\hat{\mathbf t}}{ds}. \]

This vector can be decomposed into a component normal to the surface and a component tangent to the surface:

\[ \frac{d\hat{\mathbf t}}{ds} = \mathbf k_n+\mathbf k_g. \]

Here \(\mathbf k_n\) is the normal curvature component and \(\mathbf k_g\) is the geodesic curvature component.

The normal curvature is

\[ \kappa_n = \frac{d\hat{\mathbf t}}{ds}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}. \]

It can be expressed as the ratio of the second and first fundamental forms:

\[ \boxed{ \kappa_n = \frac{ L\,du^2+2M\,du\,dv+N\,dv^2 }{ E\,du^2+2F\,du\,dv+G\,dv^2 }. } \]

Key Idea

Normal curvature depends on the direction in which one moves on the surface. At a given point, different tangent directions generally have different normal curvatures.

Curve Curvature Decomposition

Consider a sphere of radius \(R\) cut by a plane. Their intersection is a circle of radius \(\rho\) and center \(\mathbf C\). At a point \(\mathbf P\) on this circle, the circle's curvature vector points inward toward \(\mathbf C\):

\[ \boldsymbol{\kappa} = \frac{\mathbf C-\mathbf P}{\rho^2}. \]

Resolve this vector into a component normal to the sphere and a component tangent to the sphere:

\[ \boldsymbol{\kappa}_n = (\boldsymbol{\kappa}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n})\hat{\mathbf n}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\kappa}_g = \boldsymbol{\kappa}-\boldsymbol{\kappa}_n. \]

Principal Curvatures

The principal curvatures are the maximum and minimum values of the normal curvature at a point on the surface.

Let

\[ x=\frac{dv}{du}. \]

Then

\[ \kappa_n = \frac{L+2Mx+Nx^2}{E+2Fx+Gx^2}. \]

Extremizing \(\kappa_n\) with respect to the direction \(x\) leads to the characteristic equation

\[ (EG-F^2)\kappa_n^2 - (EN+GL-2MF)\kappa_n + (LN-M^2) = 0. \]

Let the two roots be

\[ \kappa_1,\qquad \kappa_2. \]

These are the principal curvatures.

The mean curvature is

\[ \boxed{ H= \frac{\kappa_1+\kappa_2}{2} = \frac{EN+GL-2MF}{2(EG-F^2)}. } \]

The Gaussian curvature is

\[ \boxed{ K= \kappa_1\kappa_2 = \frac{LN-M^2}{EG-F^2} = \frac{\Delta_{II}}{\Delta_I}. } \]

Key Idea

The principal curvatures \(\kappa_1\) and \(\kappa_2\) are the extreme values of normal curvature. Their average is the mean curvature \(H\), and their product is the Gaussian curvature \(K\).

Derivative of the Normal and Gaussian Curvature

The derivatives of the unit normal vector lie in the tangent plane. Therefore,

\[ \hat{\mathbf n}_u=a\mathbf r_u+b\mathbf r_v, \]

and

\[ \hat{\mathbf n}_v=c\mathbf r_u+d\mathbf r_v. \]

By taking inner products with \(\mathbf r_u\) and \(\mathbf r_v\), one obtains

\[ \Delta_I \hat{\mathbf n}_u = (FM-GL)\mathbf r_u + (FL-EM)\mathbf r_v, \]

and

\[ \Delta_I \hat{\mathbf n}_v = (FN-GM)\mathbf r_u + (FM-EN)\mathbf r_v. \]

These relations connect changes in the surface normal to the first and second fundamental forms.

Taking the cross product gives

\[ \Delta_I^2 (\hat{\mathbf n}_u\times\hat{\mathbf n}_v) = \Delta_I\Delta_{II} (\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ K = \frac{\Delta_{II}}{\Delta_I}. } \]

This also leads to the geometric interpretation

\[ \boxed{ K = \frac{\|\hat{\mathbf n}_u\times\hat{\mathbf n}_v\|} {\|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\|}, } \]

with sign determined by orientation.

Gauss Map

At each regular point \(P\) on a surface, the unit normal vector

\[ \hat{\mathbf n}(P) \]

defines a point on the unit sphere. The map that sends each surface point to its corresponding unit normal direction is called the Gauss map.

\[ g:S\rightarrow S^2, \qquad g(P)=\hat{\mathbf n}(P). \]

Gaussian curvature measures the local area distortion produced by the Gauss map. Informally,

\[ \boxed{ K = \frac{\text{area change under Gauss map}} {\text{area on the original surface}}. } \]

More precisely, for a sufficiently small region \(S\),

\[ \int_S K\,dA \]

measures the signed area of its image under the Gauss map.

Key Idea

Gaussian curvature can be interpreted as the rate at which the surface normal changes from point to point.

Classification of Surface Points

A point \(P\) on a surface is called regular if

\[ \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\neq \mathbf 0. \]

At a regular point, the tangent plane and unit normal are well defined.

Surface points may be classified using Gaussian curvature:

ConditionType of Point
\(K>0\)Elliptic point. The surface is locally dome-like, as on a sphere.
\(K=0\)Parabolic point. The surface is locally developable in one direction, as on a cylinder.
\(K<0\)Hyperbolic point. The surface is locally saddle-like.

Interactive Gaussian Curvature on a Torus

For a torus with major radius (R), tube radius (r), and tube angle (phi=2\pi v), the Gaussian curvature is

\[ K(v)=\frac{\cos\phi}{r\bigl(R+r\cos\phi\bigr)}. \]

The outer region has positive curvature, the inner region has negative curvature, and the top and bottom isoparametric circles have zero curvature.

Key Idea

A surface can be developed onto a plane without stretching if its Gaussian curvature is zero everywhere. Cylinders and cones are developable surfaces; spheres are not.

Gauss–Bonnet Theorem and Theorema Egregium

For fuller statements, hypotheses and proofs, see Pressley [4], §10.2 and Chapter 13.

The Gauss–Bonnet theorem relates the total Gaussian curvature of a surface to its topology. In broad terms, it connects geometry with the Euler characteristic of the surface.

Gauss's Theorema Egregium states that Gaussian curvature is invariant under local isometry. This means that Gaussian curvature can be determined entirely from measurements made on the surface itself, without reference to how the surface is embedded in three-dimensional space.

Remark

A flat sheet can be rolled into a cylinder without stretching. Hence, the plane and cylinder are locally isometric, and both have \(K=0\). A sphere cannot be flattened into a plane without stretching, because a sphere has positive Gaussian curvature.

Example

For a sphere of radius $R$, the Gaussian curvature is

\[ K=\frac{1}{R^2}. \]

Thus, a smaller sphere has larger curvature.

Coons Surface Patch

Main reference: Saxena and Sahay [1], Chapter 7. Original source: Coons [8], Section III and §3.6; further reading: Farin [5], Chapter 22.

A Coons patch constructs a surface from boundary curves. Suppose four boundary curves are given:

\[ \mathbf C_0(v),\quad \mathbf C_1(v), \quad \mathbf D_0(u),\quad \mathbf D_1(u). \]

They satisfy the corner compatibility conditions:

\[ \mathbf C_0(0)=\mathbf D_0(0),\qquad \mathbf C_0(1)=\mathbf D_1(0), \]
\[ \mathbf C_1(0)=\mathbf D_0(1),\qquad \mathbf C_1(1)=\mathbf D_1(1). \]

The bilinearly blended Coons patch is

\[ \mathbf r(u,v) = (1-u)\mathbf C_0(v)+u\mathbf C_1(v) + (1-v)\mathbf D_0(u)+v\mathbf D_1(u) - \mathbf B(u,v), \]

where the bilinear correction term is

\[ \mathbf B(u,v) = (1-u)(1-v)\mathbf P_{00} + u(1-v)\mathbf P_{10} + (1-u)v\mathbf P_{01} + uv\mathbf P_{11}. \]

The four corner points are

\[ \mathbf P_{00},\mathbf P_{10},\mathbf P_{01},\mathbf P_{11}. \]

Key Idea

A Coons patch interpolates four boundary curves. It is useful for constructing a surface when the boundary curves are known.

Surface Continuity

Further reading: Farin [5], Chapter 20, and Coons [8], §5.2, discuss compatibility and continuity between surface patches.

When two surface patches meet, it is essential to distinguish parametric continuity from geometric continuity. Parametric continuity compares derivatives of the chosen parameterizations; geometric continuity compares the shape of the resulting surface and allows a regular reparameterization. The two notions are therefore not interchangeable.

Parametric Continuity: \(C^0\), \(C^1\), and \(C^2\)

Suppose two regular patches meet along corresponding boundary curves, with compatible parameter directions and orientations.

  1. \(C^0\) continuity: corresponding points on the two boundaries coincide, so there is no gap.

  2. \(C^1\) continuity: the corresponding first partial derivatives agree along the boundary. Thus the patches have the same tangent plane, but they must also have compatible derivative magnitudes and parameter speeds. Merely sharing a tangent plane is not sufficient for \(C^1\).

  3. \(C^2\) continuity: the corresponding first and second partial derivatives agree along the boundary. This is a condition on the parameterizations, not just a statement that the visible curvatures appear equal.

Geometric Continuity: \(G^0\), \(G^1\), and \(G^2\)

Two patches have geometric continuity of order \(k\) if one patch can be reparameterized regularly near the join so that the combined parameterization has \(C^k\) continuity.

  1. \(G^0\) continuity: the patches meet along the same boundary curve. With corresponding boundary points, this is the usual positional condition \(C^0\).

  2. \(G^1\) continuity: the patches share a tangent plane, or equivalently have parallel, consistently oriented surface normals, along the join. Their derivative magnitudes and parameter speeds may differ. This removes a visible crease.

  3. \(G^2\) continuity: after a suitable smooth reparameterization, the patches have second-order contact. Their curvature behavior agrees across the join even though their raw second partial derivatives need not be equal. Matching only one scalar curvature is not, by itself, enough to establish \(G^2\).

ConditionWhat must agree?Depends on parameterization?
\(C^1\)First partial derivativesYes
\(G^1\)Tangent plane and consistent normal directionNo
\(C^2\)First and second partial derivativesYes
\(G^2\)Second-order contact after reparameterizationNo
\[ C^2 \Longrightarrow G^2, \qquad C^1 \Longrightarrow G^1, \]

but the converses are generally false. Reparameterizing a patch can change whether a join is \(C^1\) or \(C^2\) without changing its geometric shape or its \(G^1\) or \(G^2\) continuity.

CAD Interpretation

For aesthetic or class-A surface design, \(G^1\) gives a crease-free join, while \(G^2\) generally produces smoother curvature and reflection-line flow. \(C^2\) is sufficient for \(G^2\), but it is a stronger, parameterization-dependent requirement and is not necessary for a visually curvature-continuous join.
