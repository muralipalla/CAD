      Which frame defines the axis?

      Fixed axes and moving axes

      Extrinsic: use the world frame

      The X, Y, and Z axes stay fixed in space. After turning about X, the next turn about Y still uses the original world Y direction. The body’s own axes move, but they do not determine the next rotation.

      Intrinsic: use the body frame

      The x′, y′, and z′ axes are attached to the aircraft. Every turn carries all three axes with it. After turning about x′, the next turn uses the new y′ direction.

      Both views start with the body frame aligned with the world frame. These are active, right-handed rotations of the body about a common origin, acting on column vectors. A point with body coordinates \(\mathbf p_b\) has world coordinates \(\mathbf p_w=Q\mathbf p_b\).

      Try two quarter-turns. Choose the example above, keep “Same order,” and advance one step at a time. Both begin with a 90° X rotation. In the second step, intrinsic Y is now directed along world Z, while extrinsic Y stays along world Y. Then choose “Reverse order” and compare the final orientations.

      A step-by-step algorithm

      Update the orientation matrix

      1. Start with the identity matrix: \(Q_0=I\).
      2. Read the next axis \(a_i\) and its angle \(\theta_i\). Form the elementary rotation \(R_i=R_{a_i}(\theta_i)\).
      3. For an extrinsic step, multiply on the left: \(Q_i=R_iQ_{i-1}\). The world-space axis is the fixed basis vector \(\mathbf e_{a_i}\).
      4. For an intrinsic step, multiply on the right: \(Q_i=Q_{i-1}R_i\). The world-space axis is \(Q_{i-1}\mathbf e_{a_i}\).
      5. Display each body point at \(Q_i\mathbf p_b\). To animate a step, replace \(\theta_i\) by \(t\theta_i\), with \(0\le t\le1\), while keeping all earlier steps complete.

      A rotation about the moved body axis, expressed in world coordinates, is \(Q_{i-1}R_iQ_{i-1}^{T}\). Applying it to the body explains the intrinsic update:

      \[\underbrace{(Q_{i-1}R_iQ_{i-1}^{T})}_{\text{rotation about the moved axis}}Q_{i-1}=Q_{i-1}R_i.\]

      Same endpoint, different journeys

      Why reversing the order works

      Let \(A=R_x(\alpha)\), \(B=R_y(\beta)\), and \(C=R_z(\gamma)\). The arrows below indicate chronological order.

      Intrinsic X → Y → Z

      Each new rotation multiplies on the right.

      \[I\ \longrightarrow\ A\ \longrightarrow\ AB\ \longrightarrow\ ABC.\]

      Extrinsic Z → Y → X

      Each new rotation multiplies on the left.

      \[I\ \longrightarrow\ C\ \longrightarrow\ BC\ \longrightarrow\ ABC.\]
      \[\boxed{\operatorname{Intrinsic}\big(X(\alpha)\to Y(\beta)\to Z(\gamma)\big)=\operatorname{Extrinsic}\big(Z(\gamma)\to Y(\beta)\to X(\alpha)\big)}\]

      Both finish at \(ABC\). Their intermediate orientations are generally different. The result also works in the other direction, for any axis order, when you reverse the axis–angle pairs and switch the frame rule.

      Keeping the same order gives \(Q_{\mathrm{intrinsic}}=ABC\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{extrinsic}}=CBA\), which are generally unequal because 3D rotations do not commute. Special angle choices, such as all zero or only one nonzero angle, can still make them equal.

      This does not undo the rotation. To undo \(ABC\), use its inverse \(C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}\). In the equivalence comparison, the angles retain their signs and the final orientation is preserved.

      Elementary rotation matrices and comparison angle

      With \(c=\cos\theta\) and \(s=\sin\theta\),

      \[R_x(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&c&-s\\0&s&c\end{bmatrix},\quad R_y(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}c&0&s\\0&1&0\\-s&0&c\end{bmatrix},\quad R_z(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}c&-s&0\\s&c&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.\]

      The displayed difference is the smallest angle taking one current orientation to the other:

      \[\Delta=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}(Q_{\mathrm{intrinsic}}^{T}Q_{\mathrm{extrinsic}})-1}{2}\right),\qquad 0\le\Delta\le\pi.\]

      A difference of 0° means the whole body frame agrees, not just the direction of the nose.
