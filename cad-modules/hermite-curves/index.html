Cubic Hermite Interpolation

Core course reading: Saxena and Sahay [1]. Supporting treatment of polynomial curve representations: Farin [5].

A cubic Hermite curve is determined by two endpoint positions and two endpoint tangent vectors:

\[ \mathbf P_0,\quad \mathbf P_1,\quad \mathbf T_0,\quad \mathbf T_1. \]

The curve parameter is normalized to $0\le u\le1$. The four interpolation conditions are

\[ \mathbf r(0)=\mathbf P_0,\qquad \mathbf r(1)=\mathbf P_1, \]
\[ \mathbf r'(0)=\mathbf T_0,\qquad \mathbf r'(1)=\mathbf T_1. \]

The positions determine where the segment begins and ends. The tangent vectors determine its endpoint directions and its parametric speed at those endpoints.

Key Idea

Hermite data gives direct control over endpoint position and endpoint derivative. A unique polynomial of degree at most three satisfies the four conditions in each coordinate.

Deriving the Basis Functions

Write a cubic vector polynomial as

\[ \mathbf r(u)=\mathbf a u^3+\mathbf b u^2+\mathbf c u+\mathbf d. \]

Its derivative is

\[ \mathbf r'(u)=3\mathbf a u^2+2\mathbf b u+\mathbf c. \]

The conditions at $u=0$ immediately give

\[ \mathbf d=\mathbf P_0,\qquad \mathbf c=\mathbf T_0. \]

Applying the two conditions at $u=1$ and solving for the remaining coefficients gives

\[ \mathbf a=2\mathbf P_0-2\mathbf P_1+\mathbf T_0+\mathbf T_1, \]
\[ \mathbf b=-3\mathbf P_0+3\mathbf P_1-2\mathbf T_0-\mathbf T_1. \]

Grouping the result by the four geometric inputs produces the Hermite basis form.

Hermite Basis Form

\[ \mathbf r(u)=h_{00}(u)\mathbf P_0+h_{10}(u)\mathbf T_0+h_{01}(u)\mathbf P_1+h_{11}(u)\mathbf T_1, \]

where

\[ \begin{aligned} h_{00}(u)&=2u^3-3u^2+1, & h_{10}(u)&=u^3-2u^2+u,\\ h_{01}(u)&=-2u^3+3u^2, & h_{11}(u)&=u^3-u^2. \end{aligned} \]

Each basis function is therefore associated with exactly one endpoint condition. Substituting $u=0$ or $u=1$, before or after differentiating, verifies all four constraints.

Matrix Form

For computation, the same curve can be written in power-basis matrix form:

\[ \mathbf r(u)= \begin{bmatrix}u^3&u^2&u&1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2&-2&1&1\\ -3&3&-2&-1\\ 0&0&1&0\\ 1&0&0&0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf P_0\\ \mathbf P_1\\ \mathbf T_0\\ \mathbf T_1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The middle matrix is the cubic Hermite geometry matrix. Geometry is stored in the final vector, so the same basis can be evaluated independently for the $x$, $y$, and $z$ coordinates.

Properties

  1. Endpoint interpolation: the curve passes through $\mathbf P_0$ and $\mathbf P_1$.

  2. Endpoint derivative interpolation: the curve matches both specified tangent vectors, including their magnitudes.

  3. Affine invariance: applying an affine transformation to the endpoints and its linear part to the tangent vectors gives the transformed curve.

  4. Local segment control: changing one segment’s Hermite data does not directly alter distant segments in a piecewise curve.

  5. Polynomial form: a cubic Hermite segment can be converted exactly to cubic Bézier or power-basis form.

Remark

The tangent vectors are not points. Under translation, endpoint positions move but tangent vectors do not; under rotation or scaling, both are transformed by the linear part of the transformation.

Relation to Bézier Curves

A cubic Hermite segment is the same polynomial curve as the cubic Bézier segment with control points

\[ \mathbf B_0=\mathbf P_0,\qquad \mathbf B_1=\mathbf P_0+\frac{1}{3}\mathbf T_0, \]
\[ \mathbf B_2=\mathbf P_1-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf T_1,\qquad \mathbf B_3=\mathbf P_1. \]

This follows from the Bézier endpoint derivatives

\[ \mathbf r'(0)=3(\mathbf B_1-\mathbf B_0),\qquad \mathbf r'(1)=3(\mathbf B_3-\mathbf B_2). \]

Hermite form is convenient when positions and derivatives are the natural inputs. Bézier form is convenient when a control polygon is the preferred geometric interface.

Parameterization and Tangent Magnitude

The tangent magnitude affects the curve shape because $\mathbf T_0$ and $\mathbf T_1$ are derivatives with respect to the chosen parameter. Multiplying a tangent by a positive scalar preserves its direction but changes how strongly it pulls the segment near that endpoint.

If a physical or global parameter $t$ spans $t_0\le t\le t_1$, define

\[ u=\frac{t-t_0}{t_1-t_0}. \]

By the chain rule, the tangent data used by the normalized $u$-segment must be

\[ \frac{d\mathbf r}{du}=(t_1-t_0)\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}. \]

Key Idea

Tangent direction controls the departure or arrival direction; tangent magnitude also depends on parameter scale. Reparameterizing without rescaling derivatives changes the curve.

Joining Hermite Segments

Suppose segment $\mathbf r_A$ ends where segment $\mathbf r_B$ begins.

  • $C^0$ continuity: the endpoint positions agree, $\mathbf r_A(1)=\mathbf r_B(0)$.
  • $C^1$ continuity: the endpoint positions and parameter derivatives agree, $\mathbf r_A'(1)=\mathbf r_B'(0)$.
  • $G^1$ continuity: the derivatives point in the same direction, but their magnitudes may differ by a positive scale factor.

For normalized neighboring segments, $C^1$ continuity is obtained directly by assigning the same tangent vector to the shared endpoint. If the segments cover unequal parameter intervals, their normalized tangents must include the corresponding interval lengths.

Choosing Tangents

Sometimes tangents are specified by the designer. When only interpolation points are given, they can be estimated. For uniformly spaced data $\mathbf P_i$, a common centered estimate is

\[ \mathbf T_i=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf P_{i+1}-\mathbf P_{i-1}). \]

Using these tangents in consecutive cubic Hermite segments gives the familiar Catmull–Rom construction. One-sided differences or user-specified derivatives are used at the ends. For nonuniformly spaced data, derivative estimates should account for the parameter intervals rather than treating every sample as equally spaced.

Design Consideration

Large tangents can cause bulges or loops, while very small tangents flatten the curve near an endpoint. Interpolation alone does not guarantee fairness, monotonicity, or the absence of self-intersections.

Worked Example

Let

\[ \mathbf P_0=(0,0),\quad \mathbf P_1=(3,2),\quad \mathbf T_0=(3,0),\quad \mathbf T_1=(0,3). \]

At $u=\tfrac12$, the four basis values are

\[ h_{00}=\frac12,\qquad h_{10}=\frac18,\qquad h_{01}=\frac12,\qquad h_{11}=-\frac18. \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf r\!\left(\frac12\right) &=\frac12(0,0)+\frac18(3,0)+\frac12(3,2)-\frac18(0,3)\\ &=\left(\frac{15}{8},\frac{5}{8}\right). \end{aligned} \]

The midpoint in parameter is not generally the midpoint of the endpoint positions. The tangent data changes both the path and the rate at which it is traversed.

Summary

  • A cubic Hermite segment interpolates two positions and two first derivatives.
  • Four cubic basis functions isolate the four endpoint constraints.
  • The Hermite and cubic Bézier forms are exactly convertible.
  • Tangent magnitude matters and must be scaled when the parameter interval changes.
  • Shared endpoint positions give $C^0$ continuity; compatible derivatives give $C^1$ or $G^1$ continuity.
