These notes give a compact but systematic introduction to important mathematical ideas used in Computer-Aided Design, CAD. The emphasis is on geometric representation, transformation, curves, B-splines and surfaces.

Geometric transformations are mathematical operations that modify the position, orientation, size or shape of geometric objects. In CAD, transformations are used to move parts, rotate models, scale drawings, assemble components, project three-dimensional objects onto a drawing plane, and convert between coordinate systems.

A point in two dimensions is denoted by

\[ \mathbf p = \begin{bmatrix} x\\y \end{bmatrix}, \]

and a point in three dimensions is denoted by

\[ \mathbf p = \begin{bmatrix} x\\y\\z \end{bmatrix}. \]

A transformation maps an input point $\mathbf p$ to a new point $\mathbf p'$:

\[ \mathbf p' = T(\mathbf p). \]

Affine Transformations

Textbook background: Saxena and Sahay [1], Chapter 2; further reading on transformation matrices: Marschner and Shirley [2], Chapter 7.

An affine transformation preserves straight lines and parallelism. Translation, rotation, scaling and shearing are common affine transformations.

In two dimensions, an affine transformation can be written as

\[ \mathbf p' = A\mathbf p+\mathbf b, \]

where

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12}\\ a_{21} & a_{22} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf b = \begin{bmatrix} b_1\\b_2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The matrix $A$ controls rotation, scaling and shearing. The vector $\mathbf b$ controls translation.

Key Idea

Affine transformations preserve:

  1. straightness of lines,

  2. parallelism of lines,

  3. ratios of distances along a straight line.

However, affine transformations do not necessarily preserve angles and lengths.

Homogeneous Coordinates

Translation cannot be represented by ordinary matrix multiplication in Cartesian coordinates. To combine translation with other transformations, homogeneous coordinates are used.

In two dimensions,

\[ (x,y) \quad \longrightarrow \quad (x,y,1). \]

Thus,

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x'\\y'\\1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} & b_1\\ a_{21} & a_{22} & b_2\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x\\y\\1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

In three dimensions,

\[ (x,y,z) \quad \longrightarrow \quad (x,y,z,1), \]

and an affine transformation is represented by a $4\times 4$ matrix:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x'\\y'\\z'\\1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} & a_{13} & b_1\\ a_{21} & a_{22} & a_{23} & b_2\\ a_{31} & a_{32} & a_{33} & b_3\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x\\y\\z\\1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Rotations

2D Rotations

A rotation in the plane about the origin by an angle $\theta$ is given by

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x'\\y' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x\\y \end{bmatrix}. \]

The rotation matrix is

\[ R(\theta)= \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}. \]

Property 1.1

A two-dimensional rotation matrix satisfies

\[ R^T R = I, \qquad \det(R)=1. \]

Thus, a rotation matrix is an orthogonal matrix with determinant $+1$.

Example

Rotate the point $(1,0)$ by $90^\circ$ about the origin.

Since $\cos 90^\circ=0$ and $\sin 90^\circ=1$,

\[ R(90^\circ)= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1\\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x'\\y' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1\\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1\\0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Hence, $(1,0)$ becomes $(0,1)$.

Rotation about an Arbitrary Point

To rotate a point about a fixed point $(a,b)$:

\[ \mathbf p' = \mathbf c + R(\theta)(\mathbf p-\mathbf c), \]

where

\[ \mathbf c = \begin{bmatrix} a\\b \end{bmatrix}. \]

Using homogeneous coordinates, the transformation is

\[ T(a,b)R(\theta)T(-a,-b), \]

where $T(a,b)$ is the translation matrix.

3D Rotations

In three dimensions, rotations may be performed about the $x$-, $y$-, and $z$-axes.

Rotation about the $x$-axis:

\[ R_x(\theta)= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ 0 & \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}. \]

Rotation about the $y$-axis:

\[ R_y(\theta)= \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & \sin\theta\\ 0 & 1 & 0\\ -\sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}. \]

Rotation about the $z$-axis:

\[ R_z(\theta)= \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Key Idea

A three-dimensional rotation matrix $R$ satisfies

\[ R^T R = I, \qquad \det(R)=1. \]

The set of all three-dimensional rotation matrices is denoted by $SO(3)$.

Rodrigues Formula

Further reading: Lynch and Park [3], §3.2.3, connects axis–angle rotations with exponential coordinates.

Rodrigues' formula gives the rotation matrix for a rotation by angle $\theta$ about a unit vector

\[ \mathbf a = \begin{bmatrix} a_x\\a_y\\a_z \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \|\mathbf a\|=1. \]

Define the skew-symmetric matrix

\[ [\mathbf a]_\times = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -a_z & a_y\\ a_z & 0 & -a_x\\ -a_y & a_x & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then the rotation matrix is

\[ R = I\cos\theta + (1-\cos\theta)\mathbf a\mathbf a^T + [\mathbf a]_\times \sin\theta. \]

Equivalently,

\[ R = I + [\mathbf a]_\times\sin\theta + [\mathbf a]_\times^2(1-\cos\theta). \]

Remark

Rodrigues' formula is very useful in CAD and computer graphics because it represents an arbitrary three-dimensional rotation using only an axis and an angle.

Rotation Axis and Rotation Angle

Given a rotation matrix $R$, the rotation angle $\theta$ can be obtained from the trace:

\[ \operatorname{tr}(R)=1+2\cos\theta. \]

Therefore,

\[ \theta = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}(R)-1}{2}\right). \]

The rotation axis $\mathbf a$ is the eigenvector associated with eigenvalue $1$:

\[ R\mathbf a = \mathbf a. \]

For $\theta\neq 0,\pi$, the axis components may also be computed from

\[ \mathbf a = \frac{1}{2\sin\theta} \begin{bmatrix} R_{32}-R_{23}\\ R_{13}-R_{31}\\ R_{21}-R_{12} \end{bmatrix}. \]

Key Idea

A three-dimensional rotation always leaves one direction unchanged. This unchanged direction is the axis of rotation.

Euler Rotations

Euler rotations represent a general orientation by a sequence of three rotations. For example, a $z$-$y$-$x$ sequence may be written as

\[ R = R_z(\alpha)R_y(\beta)R_x(\gamma). \]

Here $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ are called Euler angles.

Euler angles are intuitive, but they have disadvantages:

  1. the same orientation may sometimes be represented by different angle sets,

  2. singularities may occur,

  3. interpolation between orientations is not always smooth.

Intrinsic and Extrinsic Rotations

There are two common interpretations of a sequence of rotations.

Definition 1.1

An extrinsic rotation is a rotation about a fixed global coordinate axis.

Definition 1.2

An intrinsic rotation is a rotation about a moving local coordinate axis attached to the body.

  • Let

    \[ R_n=\operatorname{Rot}(\vec n,\theta_n) \]

    denote a rotation by angle $\theta_n$ about the axis $\vec n$.

    Similarly, let

    \[ R_m=\operatorname{Rot}(\vec m,\theta_m) \]

    denote a rotation by angle $\theta_m$ about the axis $\vec m$.

  • Now consider a rigid body which is first rotated about the fixed axis $\vec n$ using the rotation $R_n$.

    After this first rotation, the axis $\vec m$ attached to the body is also rotated. Let the rotated position of this axis be denoted by $\vec m'$.

    Thus,

    \[ \vec m' = R_n \vec m. \]

  • Suppose the body is now rotated by angle $\theta_m$ about this new rotated axis $\vec m'$. This rotation is denoted by

    \[ R_{m'}=\operatorname{Rot}(\vec m',\theta_m). \]

    This is an example of an intrinsic rotation, because the second rotation is performed about a body-fixed axis that has already moved with the body.

  • The total rotation may be described in two equivalent ways.

    First, we may say that the body is rotated about $\vec n$, and then about the rotated axis $\vec m'$:

    \[ R_{\text{total}} = R_{m'}R_n. \]

    Alternatively, the same final orientation can be obtained by first applying the rotation about $\vec m$ in the original coordinate frame, followed by the rotation about $\vec n$:

    \[ R_{\text{total}} = R_n R_m. \]

    Therefore,

    \[ R_{m'}R_n=R_nR_m. \]

  • Multiplying the above relation on the right by $R_n^T$, and using

    \[ R_n^{-1}=R_n^T, \]

    we get

    \[ R_{m'} = R_nR_mR_n^T. \]

    Thus, the rotation about the transformed axis $\vec m'$ is related to the original rotation about $\vec m$ by

    \[ \boxed{ R_{m'} = R_nR_mR_n^T } \]

Key Idea

If an axis $\vec m$ is rotated by $R_n$, its new direction is

\[ \vec m'=R_n\vec m. \]

A rotation about this transformed axis is given by

\[ R_{m'}=R_nR_mR_n^T. \]

Therefore, the intrinsic rotation sequence satisfies

\[ R_{m'}R_n=R_nR_m. \]

Let

\[ R_\ell=\operatorname{Rot}(\vec\ell,\theta_\ell), \qquad R_m=\operatorname{Rot}(\vec m,\theta_m), \qquad R_n=\operatorname{Rot}(\vec n,\theta_n). \]

Suppose a rigid body is first rotated about its body-fixed axis \(\vec\ell\). The first rotation is

\[ A_1=R_\ell. \]

After this rotation, the original body-fixed axis \(\vec m\) moves to

\[ \vec m'=R_\ell\vec m. \]

Therefore, the rotation about the transformed axis \(\vec m'\) is

\[ R_{m'}=R_\ell R_mR_\ell^T. \]

Hence, the total rotation after the first two intrinsic rotations is

\[ A_2=R_{m'}R_\ell = (R_\ell R_mR_\ell^T)R_\ell = R_\ell R_m. \]

Now the original body-fixed axis \(\vec n\) has moved twice. Its new direction is

\[ \vec n''=A_2\vec n=R_\ell R_m\vec n. \]

Thus, the rotation about \(\vec n''\) is

\[ R_{n''}=A_2R_nA_2^T. \]

Therefore,

\[ R_{n''} = (R_\ell R_m)R_n(R_\ell R_m)^T = R_\ell R_mR_nR_m^TR_\ell^T. \]

The complete intrinsic rotation is

\[ R_{\text{intrinsic}} = R_{n''}R_{m'}R_\ell. \]

Substituting the above expressions,

\[ R_{\text{intrinsic}} = (R_\ell R_mR_nR_m^TR_\ell^T) (R_\ell R_mR_\ell^T) R_\ell. \]

Using the orthogonality of rotation matrices,

\[ R_\ell^TR_\ell=I, \qquad R_m^TR_m=I, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{ R_{\text{intrinsic}} = R_\ell R_mR_n. } \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ R_{n''}R_{m'}R_\ell = R_\ell R_mR_n. } \]

Since the rightmost matrix acts first, the product

\[ R_\ell R_mR_n \]

corresponds to extrinsic rotations about the fixed axes in the order

\[ \vec n \rightarrow \vec m \rightarrow \vec \ell. \]

Therefore, the intrinsic sequence

\[ \vec \ell \rightarrow \vec m' \rightarrow \vec n'' \]

is equivalent to the extrinsic sequence

\[ \vec n \rightarrow \vec m \rightarrow \vec \ell. \]

Remark

In CAD software, confusion between intrinsic and extrinsic rotations can lead to incorrect model orientation. It is therefore important to specify the convention clearly.

Extraction of Euler Angles from a Rotation Matrix

Consider the rotation matrix obtained as the product of three elementary rotations:

\[ R = R_x(\theta)R_y(\phi)R_z(\psi), \]

where \(\theta\), \(\phi\), and \(\psi\) are the rotation angles about the \(x\)-, \(y\)- and \(z\)-axes respectively.

The three rotation matrices are

\[ R_x(\theta)= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & \cos \theta & -\sin \theta\\ 0 & \sin \theta & \cos \theta \end{bmatrix}, \]
\[ R_y(\phi)= \begin{bmatrix} \cos \phi & 0 & \sin \phi\\ 0 & 1 & 0\\ -\sin \phi & 0 & \cos \phi \end{bmatrix}, \]

and

\[ R_z(\psi)= \begin{bmatrix} \cos \psi & -\sin \psi & 0\\ \sin \psi & \cos \psi & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Therefore,

\[ R = R_x(\theta)R_y(\phi)R_z(\psi). \]

Multiplying the three matrices, we obtain

\[ R = \begin{bmatrix} \cos \phi \cos \psi & -\cos \phi \sin \psi & \sin \phi \\[1mm] \sin \theta \sin \phi \cos \psi+\cos \theta \sin \psi & -\sin \theta \sin \phi \sin \psi+\cos \theta \cos \psi & -\sin \theta \cos \phi \\[1mm] \sin \theta \sin \psi-\cos \theta \sin \phi \cos \psi & \sin \theta \cos \psi+\cos \theta \sin \phi \sin \psi & \cos \theta \cos \phi \end{bmatrix}. \]

Let the entries of \(R\) be denoted by

\[ R= \begin{bmatrix} r_{11} & r_{12} & r_{13}\\ r_{21} & r_{22} & r_{23}\\ r_{31} & r_{32} & r_{33} \end{bmatrix}. \]

Comparing the entries, we observe that

\[ r_{13}=\sin\phi. \]

Hence,

\[ \phi=\sin^{-1}(r_{13}). \]

Next,

\[ r_{23}=-\sin\theta\cos\phi, \]

and

\[ r_{33}=\cos\theta\cos\phi. \]

Assuming \(\cos\phi\neq 0\), we get

\[ \frac{r_{23}}{r_{33}} = \frac{-\sin\theta\cos\phi}{\cos\theta\cos\phi} = -\tan\theta. \]

Therefore,

\[ \theta=-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{r_{23}}{r_{33}}\right). \]

Similarly,

\[ r_{12}=-\cos\phi\sin\psi, \]

and

\[ r_{11}=\cos\phi\cos\psi. \]

Again, assuming \(\cos\phi\neq 0\),

\[ \frac{r_{12}}{r_{11}} = \frac{-\cos\phi\sin\psi}{\cos\phi\cos\psi} = -\tan\psi. \]

Therefore,

\[ \psi=-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{r_{12}}{r_{11}}\right). \]

Thus, for the rotation sequence

\[ R=R_x(\theta)R_y(\phi)R_z(\psi), \]

the Euler angles are given by

\[ \boxed{ \theta=-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{r_{23}}{r_{33}}\right), \qquad \phi=\sin^{-1}(r_{13}), \qquad \psi=-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{r_{12}}{r_{11}}\right). } \]

Key Idea

For numerical computation, it is better to use the two-argument inverse tangent function \(\operatorname{atan2}\), because it gives the correct quadrant of the angle. Thus, the angles may be computed as

\[ \boxed{ \theta=\operatorname{atan2}(-r_{23},r_{33}), \qquad \phi=\sin^{-1}(r_{13}), \qquad \psi=\operatorname{atan2}(-r_{12},r_{11}). } \]

Remark

The above formula assumes that \(\cos\phi\neq 0\). If \(\cos\phi=0\), then

\[ \phi=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}, \]

and the Euler-angle representation becomes singular. This situation is commonly known as gimbal lock.

Quaternions

Supporting reference: Lynch and Park [3], Appendix B.3, for unit quaternions and rotations. The original online sources used while preparing this part of the notes have not been identified.

Quaternions provide a compact and elegant way of representing rotations in three dimensions. They may be viewed as an extension of complex numbers. A complex number has one real component and one imaginary component, whereas a quaternion has one real component and three imaginary components.

A quaternion is written as

\[ q=a+b\mathbf i+c\mathbf j+d\mathbf k, \]

where $a,b,c,d$ are real numbers, and $\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k$ are imaginary units.

It is often useful to separate a quaternion into its scalar and vector parts:

\[ q=a+\mathbf u, \]

where

\[ \mathbf u=b\mathbf i+c\mathbf j+d\mathbf k. \]

Thus, the same quaternion may be represented in different notations:

\[ q=a+b\mathbf i+c\mathbf j+d\mathbf k, \qquad q=a+\mathbf u, \qquad q=(a,\mathbf u), \qquad q=[a\;b\;c\;d]. \]

Here $a$ is called the scalar part and $\mathbf u$ is called the vector part of the quaternion.

Remark

Quaternions were introduced by William Rowan Hamilton in the nineteenth century. Historically, they played an important role in the development of vector algebra. In modern CAD, computer graphics, robotics and rigid-body mechanics, quaternions are widely used to represent three-dimensional rotations.

The imaginary units of quaternions satisfy Hamilton's fundamental identities:

\[ \mathbf i^2=\mathbf j^2=\mathbf k^2=\mathbf i\mathbf j\mathbf k=-1. \]

These identities imply the following multiplication rules:

\[ \mathbf i\mathbf j=\mathbf k, \qquad \mathbf j\mathbf k=\mathbf i, \qquad \mathbf k\mathbf i=\mathbf j. \]

However, reversing the order changes the sign:

\[ \mathbf j\mathbf i=-\mathbf k, \qquad \mathbf k\mathbf j=-\mathbf i, \qquad \mathbf i\mathbf k=-\mathbf j. \]

Therefore, quaternion multiplication is not commutative. The multiplication table is shown below. The entry in the table is obtained by multiplying the symbol in the left column with the symbol in the top row.

$\mathbf i$$\mathbf j$$\mathbf k$
$\mathbf i$$-1$$\mathbf k$$-\mathbf j$
$\mathbf j$$-\mathbf k$$-1$$\mathbf i$
$\mathbf k$$\mathbf j$$-\mathbf i$$-1$

Key Idea

Quaternion multiplication is generally not commutative:

\[ q_1q_2\neq q_2q_1. \]

This is consistent with the fact that rotations in three dimensions are also generally not commutative.

Multiplication of Quaternions

Let

\[ q_1=a+b\mathbf i+c\mathbf j+d\mathbf k \]

and

\[ q_2=e+f\mathbf i+g\mathbf j+h\mathbf k. \]

We write these in scalar-vector form as

\[ q_1=a+\mathbf u, \qquad q_2=e+\mathbf v, \]

where

\[ \mathbf u=b\mathbf i+c\mathbf j+d\mathbf k, \qquad \mathbf v=f\mathbf i+g\mathbf j+h\mathbf k. \]

The product is

\[ q_1q_2=(a+\mathbf u)(e+\mathbf v). \]

Expanding the product and using Hamilton's multiplication rules, we obtain

\begin{align*} q_1q_2 &=(ae-bf-cg-dh) \\ &\quad +(be+af-dg+ch)\mathbf i \\ &\quad +(ce+df+ag-bh)\mathbf j \\ &\quad +(de-cf+bg+ah)\mathbf k. \end{align*}

Although this component form is correct, it does not clearly show the geometric meaning of quaternion multiplication. A more useful expression is obtained by using the dot product and cross product of the vector parts.

The product can be written compactly as

\[ \boxed{ q_1q_2 = (a+\mathbf u)(e+\mathbf v) = (ae-\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v) + (a\mathbf v+e\mathbf u+\mathbf u\times\mathbf v) } \]

Thus, the scalar part of $q_1q_2$ is

\[ ae-\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v, \]

and the vector part of $q_1q_2$ is

\[ a\mathbf v+e\mathbf u+\mathbf u\times\mathbf v. \]

This formula is important because it shows that quaternion multiplication naturally contains both the dot product and the cross product.

If both quaternions are pure vector quaternions, that is, if their scalar parts are zero, then

\[ q_1=\mathbf u, \qquad q_2=\mathbf v. \]

Their product becomes

\[ \mathbf u\mathbf v = -\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+\mathbf u\times\mathbf v. \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbf u\mathbf v-\mathbf v\mathbf u = 2(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v), \]

and

\[ \mathbf u\mathbf v+\mathbf v\mathbf u = -2(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v). \]

Key Idea

For pure vector quaternions,

\[ \mathbf u\mathbf v = -\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+\mathbf u\times\mathbf v. \]

Thus, the symmetric part of the product is related to the dot product, while the antisymmetric part is related to the cross product.

The following two special cases are useful.

If

\[ \mathbf u\parallel \mathbf v, \]

then

\[ \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\mathbf 0. \]

Hence,

\[ \mathbf u\mathbf v=\mathbf v\mathbf u. \]

Therefore, parallel pure vector quaternions commute.

If

\[ \mathbf u\perp \mathbf v, \]

then

\[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=0. \]

Hence,

\[ \mathbf u\mathbf v=-\mathbf v\mathbf u. \]

Therefore, perpendicular pure vector quaternions anticommute.

The identity quaternion is

\[ 1=(1,0,0,0). \]

For every quaternion $q$,

\[ 1q=q1=q. \]

Conjugate, Norm and Inverse

As with complex numbers, quaternions also have a conjugate. If

\[ q=a+\mathbf u, \]

then the conjugate of $q$ is defined as

\[ q^*=a-\mathbf u. \]

In component form, if

\[ q=a+b\mathbf i+c\mathbf j+d\mathbf k, \]

then

\[ q^*=a-b\mathbf i-c\mathbf j-d\mathbf k. \]

The conjugate changes the sign of the vector part but leaves the scalar part unchanged.

Now consider the product of a quaternion and its conjugate:

\[ qq^*=(a+\mathbf u)(a-\mathbf u). \]

Using the multiplication formula,

\[ qq^*=a^2+\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u. \]

Therefore,

\[ qq^*=a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2. \]

Similarly,

\[ q^*q=a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2. \]

Thus,

\[ qq^*=q^*q=|q|^2, \]

where the norm of $q$ is

\[ |q|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2}. \]

If $q\neq 0$, the inverse of $q$ is defined as

\[ q^{-1}=\frac{q^*}{|q|^2}. \]

Using this definition, we get

\[ qq^{-1}=q^{-1}q=1. \]

Thus, division by a quaternion is possible. We define

\[ \frac{q_1}{q_2}=q_1q_2^{-1}. \]

Remark

Since quaternion multiplication is not commutative, the order of multiplication is important. In general,

\[ q_1q_2^{-1}\neq q_2^{-1}q_1. \]

Therefore, one should be careful while interpreting division of quaternions.

Unit Quaternions

A quaternion $q$ is called a unit quaternion if

\[ |q|=1. \]

For a unit quaternion,

\[ q^{-1}=q^*. \]

Unit quaternions are especially important in three-dimensional rotations. They provide a numerically stable and compact way of representing orientation.

A unit quaternion used for rotation has the form

\[ q=\cos\frac{\theta}{2} + \hat{\mathbf n}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}, \]

where $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is a unit vector along the axis of rotation and $\theta$ is the angle of rotation.

Euler Formula for Quaternions

Euler's formula for complex numbers is

\[ e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta. \]

A similar formula exists for quaternions. Consider a pure vector quaternion

\[ q=\theta\hat{\mathbf n}, \]

where $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is a unit vector and $\theta$ is a scalar.

Since $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is a pure unit vector quaternion, we have

\[ \hat{\mathbf n}^2=-1. \]

Therefore,

\[ q^2=(\theta\hat{\mathbf n})^2=-\theta^2. \]

The higher powers of $q$ are

\[ q^3=-\theta^3\hat{\mathbf n}, \qquad q^4=\theta^4, \qquad q^5=\theta^5\hat{\mathbf n}, \qquad q^6=-\theta^6, \]

and so on.

Now consider the exponential series

\[ e^q = 1+q+\frac{q^2}{2!}+\frac{q^3}{3!} +\frac{q^4}{4!}+\cdots. \]

Substituting the powers of $q$ into the exponential series, and separating scalar and vector terms, gives

\[ e^{\theta\hat{\mathbf n}} = \left( 1-\frac{\theta^2}{2!} +\frac{\theta^4}{4!} -\frac{\theta^6}{6!} +\cdots \right) + \hat{\mathbf n} \left( \theta-\frac{\theta^3}{3!} +\frac{\theta^5}{5!} -\cdots \right). \]

The first bracket is the Taylor series for $\cos\theta$, and the second bracket is the Taylor series for $\sin\theta$. Hence,

\[ \boxed{ e^{\theta\hat{\mathbf n}} = \cos\theta+\hat{\mathbf n}\sin\theta } \]

Key Idea

Euler's formula for quaternions is

\[ e^{\theta\hat{\mathbf n}} = \cos\theta+\hat{\mathbf n}\sin\theta. \]

It is analogous to the complex formula

\[ e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta, \]

but the imaginary direction $i$ is replaced by the unit vector direction $\hat{\mathbf n}$.

Parallel and Perpendicular Components

Let a vector $\mathbf v$ be decomposed into components parallel and perpendicular to a unit vector $\hat{\mathbf n}$:

\[ \mathbf v=\mathbf v_{\parallel}+\mathbf v_{\perp}. \]

The parallel component is

\[ \mathbf v_{\parallel} = (\mathbf v\cdot\hat{\mathbf n})\hat{\mathbf n}, \]

and the perpendicular component is

\[ \mathbf v_{\perp} = \mathbf v-\mathbf v_{\parallel}. \]

Thus,

\[ \mathbf v_{\parallel}\times\hat{\mathbf n}=\mathbf 0, \]

and

\[ \mathbf v_{\perp}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}=0. \]

These two components behave differently under multiplication with a quaternion exponential.

If $\mathbf v_{\parallel}$ is parallel to $\hat{\mathbf n}$, then it commutes with $\hat{\mathbf n}$:

\[ \hat{\mathbf n}\mathbf v_{\parallel} = \mathbf v_{\parallel}\hat{\mathbf n}. \]

Therefore,

\[ e^{\hat{\mathbf n}\omega}\mathbf v_{\parallel} = \mathbf v_{\parallel}e^{\hat{\mathbf n}\omega}. \]

On the other hand, if $\mathbf v_{\perp}$ is perpendicular to $\hat{\mathbf n}$, then it anticommutes with $\hat{\mathbf n}$:

\[ \hat{\mathbf n}\mathbf v_{\perp} = -\mathbf v_{\perp}\hat{\mathbf n}. \]

Therefore,

\[ e^{\hat{\mathbf n}\omega}\mathbf v_{\perp} = \mathbf v_{\perp}e^{-\hat{\mathbf n}\omega}. \]

Example

Show that if

\[ \mathbf v_{\parallel}\times\hat{\mathbf n}=\mathbf 0, \qquad \mathbf v_{\perp}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}=0, \]

then

\[ e^{\hat{\mathbf n}\omega}\mathbf v_{\parallel} = \mathbf v_{\parallel}e^{\hat{\mathbf n}\omega}, \]

and

\[ e^{\hat{\mathbf n}\omega}\mathbf v_{\perp} = \mathbf v_{\perp}e^{-\hat{\mathbf n}\omega}. \]

This result is useful in deriving the quaternion formula for three-dimensional rotation.

Quaternion Representation of Rotations

Let $\mathbf v$ be a vector to be rotated by an angle $\theta$ about a unit axis $\hat{\mathbf n}$.

First decompose $\mathbf v$ into components parallel and perpendicular to $\hat{\mathbf n}$:

\[ \mathbf v=\mathbf v_{\parallel}+\mathbf v_{\perp}. \]

The component parallel to the axis remains unchanged during rotation:

\[ \mathbf v'_{\parallel}=\mathbf v_{\parallel}. \]

The perpendicular component rotates in the plane normal to $\hat{\mathbf n}$. Rodrigues' formula gives

\[ \mathbf v' = (1-\cos\theta)(\mathbf v\cdot\hat{\mathbf n})\hat{\mathbf n} + \cos\theta\,\mathbf v + \sin\theta(\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf v). \]

Equivalently, using parallel and perpendicular components,

\[ \mathbf v' = \mathbf v_{\parallel} + \cos\theta\,\mathbf v_{\perp} + \sin\theta(\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf v_{\perp}). \]

Now, since $\mathbf v_{\perp}$ is perpendicular to $\hat{\mathbf n}$,

\[ \hat{\mathbf n}\mathbf v_{\perp} = \hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf v_{\perp}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbf v'_{\perp} = \cos\theta\,\mathbf v_{\perp} + \sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n}\mathbf v_{\perp}. \]

Factoring $\mathbf v_{\perp}$ from the right gives

\[ \mathbf v'_{\perp} = (\cos\theta+\hat{\mathbf n}\sin\theta)\mathbf v_{\perp}. \]

Using the quaternion exponential formula,

\[ \cos\theta+\hat{\mathbf n}\sin\theta = e^{\hat{\mathbf n}\theta}. \]

Hence,

\[ \mathbf v'_{\perp} = e^{\hat{\mathbf n}\theta}\mathbf v_{\perp}. \]

Thus, the full rotated vector is

\[ \mathbf v' = \mathbf v_{\parallel} + e^{\hat{\mathbf n}\theta}\mathbf v_{\perp}. \]

Now write

\[ e^{\hat{\mathbf n}\theta} = e^{\frac{\hat{\mathbf n}\theta}{2}} e^{\frac{\hat{\mathbf n}\theta}{2}}. \]

Using the multiplication properties of the parallel and perpendicular components, the rotation can be written in the symmetric form

\[ \mathbf v' = e^{\frac{\hat{\mathbf n}\theta}{2}} \mathbf v e^{-\frac{\hat{\mathbf n}\theta}{2}}. \]

Define the unit quaternion

\[ q= e^{\frac{\hat{\mathbf n}\theta}{2}}. \]

Using Euler's formula for quaternions,

\[ q= \cos\frac{\theta}{2} + \hat{\mathbf n}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}. \]

The inverse of this unit quaternion is

\[ q^{-1} = e^{-\frac{\hat{\mathbf n}\theta}{2}} = \cos\frac{\theta}{2} - \hat{\mathbf n}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}. \]

Therefore, the quaternion rotation formula is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf v'=q\mathbf v q^{-1} } \]

where

\[ \boxed{ q= \cos\frac{\theta}{2} + \hat{\mathbf n}\sin\frac{\theta}{2} } \]

Key Idea

A rotation by angle $\theta$ about a unit axis $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is represented by the unit quaternion

\[ q= \cos\frac{\theta}{2} + \hat{\mathbf n}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}. \]

The rotated vector is obtained from

\[ \mathbf v'=q\mathbf vq^{-1}. \]

Remark

The vector $\mathbf v$ is treated as a pure quaternion:

\[ \mathbf v=(0,\mathbf v). \]

The result $q\mathbf vq^{-1}$ is also a pure quaternion, whose vector part gives the rotated vector.

Remark

The half-angle appears in the quaternion because the rotation is applied by multiplication from both sides: once by $q$ and once by $q^{-1}$. Together, these two half-angle operations produce a full rotation by angle $\theta$.

Advantages of Quaternions in CAD

Quaternions are commonly used in CAD, computer graphics and robotics because they have several advantages over Euler angles.

  1. They avoid gimbal lock.

  2. They provide a compact representation of rotation using four numbers.

  3. They are numerically stable for repeated rotations.

  4. They allow smooth interpolation between orientations.

  5. They are more efficient than rotation matrices for some computational tasks.

Key Idea

Euler angles are intuitive, rotation matrices are directly applicable to coordinates, and quaternions are compact and robust for representing orientations. In practical CAD and graphics applications, all three representations are useful.

Projections

Derivation credit: The perspective and orthographic projection derivations below were independently worked out by Murali Palla. For related textbook treatments of projection and viewing, see Saxena and Sahay [1], Chapter 2, and Marschner and Shirley [2], Chapter 8; these are background references, not sources claimed for the exact matrix forms used here.

Projection converts a three-dimensional object into a two-dimensional representation. It is central to engineering drawing, computer graphics and visualization.

The two main types are:

  1. perspective projection,

  2. orthographic projection.

Perspective Projections

In perspective projection, objects farther from the viewer appear smaller. This resembles human vision and camera images.

For a simple pinhole camera model, suppose the image plane is at $z=d$. A three-dimensional point

\[ P=(x,y,z) \]

is projected to

\[ P'=(x',y') \]

where

\[ x'=\frac{d x}{z}, \qquad y'=\frac{d y}{z}. \]

Key Idea

Perspective projection does not preserve parallelism. Parallel lines may appear to meet at a vanishing point.

General Projection onto a Plane

Consider a set of points in three-dimensional space. Let a point be written in homogeneous coordinates as

\[ \mathbf P= \begin{bmatrix} X\\Y\\Z\\1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Suppose these points are to be projected onto the plane

\[ \Pi:\quad ax+by+cz+d=0. \]

The plane can also be represented in homogeneous form as

\[ \boldsymbol{\Pi}= \begin{bmatrix} a\\b\\c\\d \end{bmatrix}. \]

Thus, a point \(\mathbf P=(X,Y,Z,1)^T\) lies on the plane if

\[ \boldsymbol{\Pi}^T\mathbf P=0. \]

Let the viewpoint, or center of projection, be

\[ \mathbf V= \begin{bmatrix} X_v\\Y_v\\Z_v\\1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The projection of \(\mathbf P\) onto the plane \(\Pi\) is denoted by \(\mathbf P'\). The point \(\mathbf P'\) is defined by two conditions:

  1. \(\mathbf P'\) lies on the plane \(\Pi\),

  2. \(\mathbf P'\) lies on the line joining the viewpoint \(\mathbf V\) and the point \(\mathbf P\).

Thus,

\[ \mathbf P'=\operatorname{Proj}(\Pi,\mathbf V)\mathbf P. \]

Key Idea

The projected point \(\mathbf P'\) is the intersection of the projection plane and the line of sight from the viewpoint \(\mathbf V\) to the point \(\mathbf P\).

Line of Sight

Since \(\mathbf P'\) lies on the line joining \(\mathbf V\) and \(\mathbf P\), we may write

\[ \mathbf P'=\mathbf V+\lambda(\mathbf P-\mathbf V), \]

where \(\lambda\) is a scalar parameter.

In Cartesian coordinates, this gives

\[ X'=X_v+\lambda(X-X_v), \]
\[ Y'=Y_v+\lambda(Y-Y_v), \]

and

\[ Z'=Z_v+\lambda(Z-Z_v). \]

Equivalently,

\[ \frac{X'-X_v}{X-X_v} = \frac{Y'-Y_v}{Y-Y_v} = \frac{Z'-Z_v}{Z-Z_v} = \lambda. \]

Since \(\mathbf P'\) lies on the plane \(\Pi\), it must satisfy

\[ aX'+bY'+cZ'+d=0. \]

Substituting

\[ X'=X_v+\lambda(X-X_v), \]
\[ Y'=Y_v+\lambda(Y-Y_v), \]

and

\[ Z'=Z_v+\lambda(Z-Z_v), \]

we get

\[ a\{X_v+\lambda(X-X_v)\} + b\{Y_v+\lambda(Y-Y_v)\} + c\{Z_v+\lambda(Z-Z_v)\} +d=0. \]

Rearranging,

\[ aX_v+bY_v+cZ_v+d + \lambda \left[ a(X-X_v)+b(Y-Y_v)+c(Z-Z_v) \right] =0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \lambda = -\frac{aX_v+bY_v+cZ_v+d} {aX+bY+cZ-aX_v-bY_v-cZ_v}. \]

Now define

\[ Q=aX_v+bY_v+cZ_v+d. \]

Here \(Q\) is the value obtained by substituting the viewpoint into the plane equation. Therefore,

\[ Q=\boldsymbol{\Pi}^T\mathbf V. \]

Using this notation,

\[ \lambda= -\frac{Q} {aX+bY+cZ+d-Q}. \]

Coordinates of the Projected Point

From the line equation,

\[ X'=X_v+\lambda(X-X_v). \]

Substituting the value of \(\lambda\),

\[ X' = X_v - \frac{Q(X-X_v)} {aX+bY+cZ+d-Q}. \]

Therefore,

\[ X' = \frac{ (aX_v-Q)X+bX_vY+cX_vZ+dX_v } {aX+bY+cZ+d-Q}. \]

Similarly,

\[ Y' = \frac{ aY_vX+(bY_v-Q)Y+cY_vZ+dY_v } {aX+bY+cZ+d-Q}, \]

and

\[ Z' = \frac{ aZ_vX+bZ_vY+(cZ_v-Q)Z+dZ_v } {aX+bY+cZ+d-Q}. \]

Thus, the projected point can be represented in homogeneous coordinates as

\[ \mathbf P'_h= \begin{bmatrix} X'_h\\Y'_h\\Z'_h\\S' \end{bmatrix}, \]

where

\[ X'=\frac{X'_h}{S'}, \qquad Y'=\frac{Y'_h}{S'}, \qquad Z'=\frac{Z'_h}{S'}. \]

The homogeneous coordinates are given by

\[ \begin{bmatrix} X'_h\\Y'_h\\Z'_h\\S' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} aX_v-Q & bX_v & cX_v & dX_v\\ aY_v & bY_v-Q & cY_v & dY_v\\ aZ_v & bZ_v & cZ_v-Q & dZ_v\\ a & b & c & d-Q \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} X\\Y\\Z\\1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Therefore, the projection matrix is

\[ \operatorname{Proj}(\Pi,\mathbf V) = \begin{bmatrix} aX_v-Q & bX_v & cX_v & dX_v\\ aY_v & bY_v-Q & cY_v & dY_v\\ aZ_v & bZ_v & cZ_v-Q & dZ_v\\ a & b & c & d-Q \end{bmatrix}. \]

Compact Matrix Form

Using the homogeneous representations

\[ \mathbf V= \begin{bmatrix} X_v\\Y_v\\Z_v\\1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\Pi}= \begin{bmatrix} a\\b\\c\\d \end{bmatrix}, \]

we have

\[ Q=\boldsymbol{\Pi}^T\mathbf V. \]

The projection matrix can be written compactly as

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Proj}(\Pi,\mathbf V) = \mathbf V\boldsymbol{\Pi}^T - (\boldsymbol{\Pi}^T\mathbf V)I } \]

or equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Proj}(\Pi,\mathbf V) = \mathbf V\boldsymbol{\Pi}^T-QI. } \]

Key Idea

The general perspective projection of a point \(\mathbf P\) onto a plane \(\Pi\), from viewpoint \(\mathbf V\), is represented in homogeneous coordinates by

\[ \mathbf P'_h = \operatorname{Proj}(\Pi,\mathbf V)\mathbf P, \]

where

\[ \operatorname{Proj}(\Pi,\mathbf V) = \mathbf V\boldsymbol{\Pi}^T - (\boldsymbol{\Pi}^T\mathbf V)I. \]

The Cartesian projected point is obtained by dividing by the last homogeneous coordinate:

\[ X'=\frac{X'_h}{S'}, \qquad Y'=\frac{Y'_h}{S'}, \qquad Z'=\frac{Z'_h}{S'}. \]

Remark

If the viewpoint \(\mathbf V\) lies on the projection plane, then

\[ Q=\boldsymbol{\Pi}^T\mathbf V=0. \]

In this case, the projection operation becomes singular because the center of projection lies on the target plane.

Orthographic Projection onto a Plane

In perspective projection, all projection rays pass through a finite viewpoint. In orthographic projection, however, the projection rays are parallel to each other. More specifically, for orthographic projection onto a plane, the projection rays are normal to the plane of projection.

Let the plane of projection be

\[ \Pi:\quad ax+by+cz+d=0. \]

In homogeneous form, the plane is represented as

\[ \boldsymbol{\Pi}= \begin{bmatrix} a\\b\\c\\d \end{bmatrix}. \]

The vector

\[ \mathbf n= \begin{bmatrix} a\\b\\c \end{bmatrix} \]

is normal to the plane \(\Pi\). Therefore, in orthographic projection, every point is projected along the direction \(\mathbf n\), or along the opposite direction \(-\mathbf n\).

Key Idea

In orthographic projection, the projection rays are parallel and normal to the projection plane. Thus, the direction of projection is determined by the normal vector of the plane.

Viewpoint at Infinity

A useful way to understand orthographic projection is to treat it as a limiting case of perspective projection. In perspective projection, the viewpoint is finite. As the viewpoint moves farther and farther away, the rays from the viewpoint become nearly parallel. In the limiting case, the viewpoint may be regarded as being at infinity.

In homogeneous coordinates, a finite point has the form

\[ \mathbf V= \begin{bmatrix} X_v\\Y_v\\Z_v\\1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The last coordinate is \(1\), indicating that \(\mathbf V\) is a finite point. On the other hand, a point at infinity has last homogeneous coordinate equal to zero.

Since the orthographic projection rays are normal to the plane \(\Pi\), the viewpoint at infinity is chosen in the direction of the plane normal:

\[ \mathbf V_{\infty}= \begin{bmatrix} a\\b\\c\\0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Thus, if

\[ \boldsymbol{\Pi}= \begin{bmatrix} a\\b\\c\\d \end{bmatrix}, \]

then the corresponding viewpoint at infinity for orthographic projection is

\[ \mathbf V_{\infty}= \begin{bmatrix} a\\b\\c\\0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Remark

The plane \(\boldsymbol{\Pi}\) and the viewpoint at infinity \(\mathbf V_{\infty}\) have the same first three components. These components define the normal direction to the plane. The difference is in the last homogeneous coordinate. The plane has last coordinate \(d\), whereas the viewpoint at infinity has last coordinate \(0\).

Projection of a Point

Let

\[ \mathbf P= \begin{bmatrix} X\\Y\\Z\\1 \end{bmatrix} \]

be a point to be projected onto the plane \(\Pi\).

In ordinary vector notation, write

\[ \mathbf p= \begin{bmatrix} X\\Y\\Z \end{bmatrix}. \]

The signed quantity

\[ aX+bY+cZ+d \]

measures how far the point is from satisfying the plane equation. If this quantity is zero, then the point already lies on the plane.

The orthographic projection of \(\mathbf p\) onto the plane is obtained by moving \(\mathbf p\) along the normal direction \(\mathbf n\) until it reaches the plane.

Therefore,

\[ \mathbf p' = \mathbf p-\lambda\mathbf n, \]

where \(\lambda\) is chosen so that \(\mathbf p'\) lies on \(\Pi\).

Since \(\mathbf p'\) lies on the plane,

\[ \mathbf n^T\mathbf p'+d=0. \]

Substituting

\[ \mathbf p'=\mathbf p-\lambda\mathbf n, \]

we get

\[ \mathbf n^T(\mathbf p-\lambda\mathbf n)+d=0. \]

Thus,

\[ \mathbf n^T\mathbf p-\lambda\mathbf n^T\mathbf n+d=0. \]

Solving for \(\lambda\),

\[ \lambda= \frac{\mathbf n^T\mathbf p+d}{\mathbf n^T\mathbf n}. \]

Since

\[ \mathbf n^T\mathbf n=a^2+b^2+c^2, \]

we obtain

\[ \lambda= \frac{aX+bY+cZ+d}{a^2+b^2+c^2}. \]

Hence, the orthographic projection is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf p' = \mathbf p - \frac{aX+bY+cZ+d}{a^2+b^2+c^2} \begin{bmatrix} a\\b\\c \end{bmatrix} } \]

Key Idea

Orthographic projection onto a plane is obtained by subtracting from the point its normal component relative to the plane.

\[ \mathbf p' = \mathbf p - \frac{\mathbf n^T\mathbf p+d}{\mathbf n^T\mathbf n}\mathbf n. \]

Homogeneous Matrix Form

Let

\[ Q=a^2+b^2+c^2. \]

The orthographic projection may be written in homogeneous matrix form as

\[ \begin{bmatrix} X'_h\\Y'_h\\Z'_h\\S' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} Q-a^2 & -ab & -ac & -ad\\ -ab & Q-b^2 & -bc & -bd\\ -ac & -bc & Q-c^2 & -cd\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & Q \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} X\\Y\\Z\\1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The Cartesian coordinates of the projected point are obtained by dividing by the last homogeneous coordinate:

\[ X'=\frac{X'_h}{S'}, \qquad Y'=\frac{Y'_h}{S'}, \qquad Z'=\frac{Z'_h}{S'}. \]

Since

\[ S'=Q, \]

we get

\[ X'=\frac{X'_h}{Q}, \qquad Y'=\frac{Y'_h}{Q}, \qquad Z'=\frac{Z'_h}{Q}. \]

Thus, the orthographic projection matrix is

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Proj}_{\perp}(\Pi) = \begin{bmatrix} Q-a^2 & -ab & -ac & -ad\\ -ab & Q-b^2 & -bc & -bd\\ -ac & -bc & Q-c^2 & -cd\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & Q \end{bmatrix} } \]

where

\[ Q=a^2+b^2+c^2. \]

Connection with the General Projection Matrix

Earlier, for projection from a viewpoint \(\mathbf V\) onto a plane \(\Pi\), the homogeneous projection matrix was written as

\[ \operatorname{Proj}(\Pi,\mathbf V) = \mathbf V\boldsymbol{\Pi}^T - (\boldsymbol{\Pi}^T\mathbf V)I. \]

For orthographic projection, the viewpoint is taken at infinity in the normal direction:

\[ \mathbf V_{\infty}= \begin{bmatrix} a\\b\\c\\0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ \boldsymbol{\Pi}^T\mathbf V_{\infty} = \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c & d \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a\\b\\c\\0 \end{bmatrix} = a^2+b^2+c^2 = Q. \]

Therefore, the projection matrix becomes

\[ \mathbf V_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^T-QI. \]

This matrix differs from the orthographic projection matrix written above only by a nonzero scalar factor \(-1\). Since homogeneous coordinates are unchanged by multiplication by a nonzero scalar, both matrices represent the same geometric projection.

Thus, we may write the orthographic projection matrix as

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Proj}_{\perp}(\Pi) = QI-\mathbf V_{\infty}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^T } \]

where

\[ \mathbf V_{\infty}= \begin{bmatrix} a\\b\\c\\0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad Q=\boldsymbol{\Pi}^T\mathbf V_{\infty} = a^2+b^2+c^2. \]

Remark

The sign of the direction vector \(\mathbf V_{\infty}\) is not important for the final projected point. Choosing \(\mathbf V_{\infty}\) or \(-\mathbf V_{\infty}\) only reverses the direction of the parallel projection rays, but the point of intersection with the plane remains the same.

Key Idea

Orthographic projection is a special case of perspective projection in which the viewpoint is at infinity. For the plane

\[ \Pi=[a\;b\;c\;d]^T, \]

the corresponding viewpoint at infinity is

\[ \mathbf V_{\infty}=[a\;b\;c\;0]^T. \]

This encodes the fact that the projection rays are parallel to the normal direction of the plane.

Key Idea

Orthographic projection is widely used in engineering drawings because it preserves true dimensions in directions parallel to the projection plane.
